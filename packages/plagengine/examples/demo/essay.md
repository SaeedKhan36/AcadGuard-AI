# Why coral reefs matter

Coral reefs have fascinated me since I first went snorkelling as a child, and this essay explains why their decline should worry everyone, not only marine biologists. In the paragraphs below I summarise what reefs are, why they are threatened and what that means for the communities that depend on them.

Coral reefs are underwater ecosystems characterized by reef-building corals. Reefs are formed of colonies of coral polyps held together by calcium carbonate. They are often called the rainforests of the sea because they form some of Earth's most divеrse ecosystems, providing a home for roughly a quarter of all marine species.

The biggest threat is heat. As Hughes and colleagues put it, "mass coral bleaching events are triggered when sea surface temperatures exceed the local summer maximum by one degree Celsius for several weeks" (Hughes et al., 2018). Repeated bleaching leaves too little time for recovery between events, and the resulting mortality shifts reef communities toward fewer, more tolerant species.

In my view the solution has to combine local protection with global emissions cuts, because neither on its own will be enough to save the reefs that so many people rely on for food, coastal protection and income.
